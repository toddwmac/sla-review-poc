1 · Purpose of This Document

One entry point that answers three questions: what was done, what documents exist, and what the review concluded.

The revised USU–AGI SLA was assessed by four different AI systems working independently, then compared in three cross-model comparison reports, and fused by the fusion engine into a final Board brief. That process produced 31 repository files across the workflow layers — a volume of material that is difficult to navigate. This master view organizes it for Board use.

New to the material? The review roadmap shows the six-stage flow with the workflow diagram, and the workflow narrative tells the full story of how the layers fed each other.

One-paragraph bottom line All four independent assessments agree that Draft 2.0 is a sound governance framework that is not yet an executable operating agreement. They strongly agree on what needs fixing — decision rights, measurable service standards, escalation clocks, data exit, employer verification, and system inventory — but differ on how, when (before signature vs. before effectiveness), and on a handful of governance choices. The consensus recommendation is to endorse the framework in principle, but do not authorize signature until a defined pre-signature worklist is complete.

Why two layers of AI cross-checking? Stage 2 asked three models to compare the four assessments; Stage 3 then asked those same three models to compare each other’s comparisons. 5 · What the Review Concluded

The substance at Board altitude, with pointers to the detail.

5.1 · Where all four assessments agree (strong consensus)

The reviews independently converged on the same six needs. Agreement is on what must be clarified — not on exact wording, thresholds, or deadlines.

  1. Decision rights. Specify who at USU approves, validates, executes, and monitors material decisions — with thresholds and a delegation schedule.
  2. Measurable service standards. Section 17 lists evidence categories, not numeric targets. Evidence that work occurred is not a performance standard.
  3. Escalation and incident clocks. Replace “promptly” / “immediately” with severity levels and fixed notice deadlines.
  4. Data return and exit. Make transition obligations testable: export formats, timelines, assistance, certified destruction.
  5. Employer-of-record register. Verify the legal employer for every covered position before execution.
  6. System inventory. Resolve undefined names (notably “CNS”) in a controlled appendix with owners and data classifications.

5.2 · Where several reviews align (moderate consensus)

  • Commercial and legal terms (3 of 4 — ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity): the draft lacks term, renewal, termination, liability, insurance, dispute resolution, and a documented shared-cost allocation methodology.
  • Board oversight (3 of 4): Audit Committee cadence, rating definitions, named owner rosters, Board sign-off on the agreement itself.
  • Independence optics (3 of 4): the org chart appears to place USU validators under the AGI CEO line; letters contain promotional claims unsuited to an accreditation-facing document.
  • Vendor flow-down controls (3 of 4) and AI / algorithmic governance (2 of 4 — the CRM “next-best-action” algorithm is described in the CEO letter but unregulated in the operative text).

5.3 · Where the reviews disagree (the open choices for the Board)

#Open choiceThe disagreement
1 When does the security-notice clock start?
clearest direct conflict		 Perplexity: within 24 h of a confirmed incident · ChatGPT: do not wait for confirmation · Copilot: 24 h window, trigger unspecified
2 What must exist before signature vs. before effectiveness? Perplexity: no signature until the metrics exhibit is approved · ChatGPT: completed schedules gate effectiveness (signing earlier is conceivable) · Copilot: metrics needed, but no pre-signature condition
3 How is “material” defined? One agreement-wide definition (Perplexity) vs. domain-specific thresholds for spending, technology, outsourcing (Claude, Copilot)
4 How does the Board evidence approval? Board Chair signature or secretary attestation (Perplexity) vs. resolution with delegated execution (ChatGPT) — compatible, but the Board must choose
5 How serious are the gaps? (severity, not substance) Copilot: “fundamentally well designed; most remaining risks are operational precision.” ChatGPT / Claude / Perplexity: the missing term, exit, and legal provisions are structural omissions. The majority view is treated as the safer reading.

5.4 · Items raised by only one review (worth considering, not consensus)

Conflict-of-interest and recusal rules for trustees with AGI roles (Claude) · privilege, independent counsel, whistleblower routing (Perplexity) · insurance minimums (Perplexity) · recovery time / point objectives for continuity (ChatGPT) · promotional claims in executive letters (Claude) · optional service credits for persistent SLA failure (ChatGPT — others silent, neither endorsement nor rejection).

5.5 · Recommended disposition (from the final Board brief)

Recommendation Endorse the framework in principle only — do not authorize signature or effectiveness yet.

Before signing

  1. Confirm authority — settle reserved decisions, delegations, materiality thresholds, and the required Board action.
  2. Approve the operating schedules — finalize the metrics exhibit, incident protocol, and service-owner roster; verify the employer register and system inventory.
  3. Confirm commercial and exit terms — document cost allocation, termination rights, and transition obligations in the SLA, or precisely identify the existing agreement that governs them (check the 2017 acquisition / intercompany agreements first).
  4. Record the approval requirements — owner, approver, and evidence of completion for each item; separate signing requirements from conditions to effectiveness.

7 · Caveats and Limitations

Carry these five qualifications into any Board discussion.

  1. Nothing was independently verified. No stage reviewed the underlying SLA text against source agreements, nor checked external regulatory requirements (WSCUC, U.S. ED, FERPA, state boards). Every finding is a claim about what the reviews say — not a verified fact about the contract or the law.
  2. Silence ≠ agreement. Review depth varies nearly 3× (10–28 items). Where a review does not mention a term, that reflects scope compression, not a position.
  3. “Unanimous” was corrected. One Stage 2 comparison report labeled several implementation details unanimous; the fusion engine traced them back and found direct conflicts (notice trigger, signing gate, review cadence). The corrected, attributed-support framing is used in §5 and in the final brief.
  4. Different priority scales. Critical / High / Medium / Low labels come from four different rubrics; they indicate reviewer emphasis, not a common severity standard.
  5. AI output is not legal advice. All documents say so explicitly. External-compliance assertions (e.g., specific WSCUC expectations, CDR thresholds, the 2017 agreements) must be verified with counsel and compliance before the Board relies on them.
