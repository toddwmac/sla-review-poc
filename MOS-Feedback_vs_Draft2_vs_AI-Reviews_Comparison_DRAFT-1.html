1 · Executive Summary

The three layers of review tell one coherent story:

Bottom line The MOS review was a governance-boundary review — who controls what. Draft 2.0 incorporated it thoroughly, in many places nearly verbatim. The four AI reviews, conducted six weeks later and without knowledge of the MOS cycle, independently confirm that the governance layer landed: their “strengths to preserve” lists describe exactly the provisions MOS required. But the AI reviews probe a different question — is the agreement executable? — and there they find the next generation of gaps: numeric targets still “pending approval,” escalation and incident clocks undefined, exit obligations untestable, and commercial/legal terms absent. These are mostly beyond the scope MOS was asked to review, with five specific threads (below) where a MOS concern remains genuinely open in Draft 2.0.

Validation point for the Board The AI reviewers never saw the MOS comments — they reviewed Draft 2.0 cold. That an independent four-model panel listed, as strengths, the very provisions MOS forced into the document (reserved-authority statement, consolidated matrices, the performance-standards framework, the employer-register gate) is genuine, unbiased evidence that the MOS revision cycle worked. The AI findings are not a contradiction of that; they are the next layer of maturity — from governance framework to executable contract.

2 · The Review Lineage

Sept 12, 2026 — original draft receives 19 reviewer comments (MOS1–MOS19): governance, accreditation, and control-language corrections
▼  Oct 14, 2026 — Draft 2.0 issued: “incorporated comments, consolidated service matrices, clarified retained authority, added performance and escalation provisions”  ▼  Nov 7, 2026 — four AI systems review Draft 2.0 independently (80 items)  ▼  Nov 20, 2026 — syntheses, fusion panel, final Comparative Board Brief

The MOS comments were driven by accreditation and fiduciary logic (WSCUC institutional-control expectations); the AI reviews by executability logic (can two organizations operate, measure, escalate, and exit under this document). Draft 2.0 sits at the boundary: it completed the accreditation-control layer and exposed the operational-contract layer.

4.2 · What the AI reviews found that MOS never raised (new ground)

The MOS review was scoped to governance boundaries. The following — all prominent in the AI consensus — have no MOS antecedent:

  • Commercial and legal architecture: term, renewal, termination, liability, indemnity, insurance, dispute resolution, governing law (R07, R19, S03, F-01 — 3-of-4 support; the majority treats missing term/exit as structural, against Copilot’s “operational precision” characterization)
  • Escalation clocks and the incident-notice trigger conflict (“confirmed” vs. “do not wait for confirmation” — F-12 vs. R04)
  • Board approval mechanics for the SLA itself (signature blocks currently AGI CEO + USU President only: R23, S17, F-05/F-19)
  • AI/algorithmic governance (the CRM next-best-action algorithm: R24, S12)
  • Conflict-of-interest and recusal rules (S05), independent counsel/privilege/whistleblower routing (F-13 — noting v2 assigns whistleblower-hotline administration to AGI under Legal Support, which sharpens Perplexity’s independence concern)
  • Service credits / financial remedies (R17, uncontested), RTO/RPO continuity objectives (R21), non-operative labeling of the executive letters’ promotional claims (R22, S16)

4.3 · Where the layers agree

All three layers converge on the same theory of the document: USU’s reserved authority is real and must be operational. MOS supplied the control language; Draft 2.0 placed it; the AI reviews ask that it be made measurable, clocked, and exit-able. There is no layer-to-layer contradiction on governance substance — the only direct contradiction within the corpus (Copilot’s “operational precision” vs. the majority’s “structural omissions”) concerns severity of the post-MOS gaps, not the MOS fixes themselves.

4.4 · Sequencing note for the Board

Draft 2.0’s own Approvals page — “Approval dates, service standards, employer assignments, and financial delegation parameters must be confirmed before execution” — is an internal pre-execution gate that pre-figures the AI reviews’ worklist. The open question the fusion panel preserved is whether that gate binds signature (Perplexity) or only effectiveness (ChatGPT’s reading), and whether the Board signs, attests, or delegates. Those are decisions, not drafting gaps, and they belong to the Board.

6 · Caveats

  1. Draft 2.0’s text is the authority for disposition. Assessments of “closed” are based on the language in the Oct 14, 2026 PDF, read against each MOS ask. This is a documentary comparison, not a legal opinion.
  2. MOS17 could not be assessed because the board summary does not itemize it. Confirm against the original comment log.
  3. The board summary is itself an AI-prepared condensation (filename indicates Claude; dated Sept 17, 2026). If the underlying 19 comments contain nuance the summary drops, dispositions could shift.
  4. AI review claims remain unverified — none of the reviews checked the SLA against the 2017 acquisition/intercompany agreements or external regulation (WSCUC, U.S. ED). Statements like “WSCUC reviewers expect a documented exit plan” are Perplexity’s assertions, flagged as such by the Haiku synthesis.
  5. Counts and IDs follow each source’s own scales; “Critical/High” totals mix four rubrics and indicate emphasis, not a common severity standard.
